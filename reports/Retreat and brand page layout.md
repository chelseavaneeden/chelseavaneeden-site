# Move proof and price up the page

*Research for Chelsea van Eeden, 8 November 2026. The question: how should the Retreats & collabs page be laid out, and what should it show, so that retreat hosts, festival and event organisers, venues, corporate teams and brands quickly find what they need, stay, and request a proposal? And should brands and retreat hosts share one page?*

Keep one page for retreat hosts, festivals, venues and teams, give brands a path of their own, and rebuild the upper half of the page around the four things every host checks before they get in touch: will your teaching suit their guests, are you qualified and insured, who else has hired you, and what will it cost and involve. Your page already does several things most competitors miss: a teaching video at the top, one clear action, a short form that sorts the request, a 48-hour reply promise and a published starting price. Two of the four checks are weak, though. There are no words from past hosts and no photos of past events, and the "from €150" price is tucked inside an FAQ answer at the very bottom of the page, far below where most visitors stop looking (in eye-tracking research, **74% of viewing time goes to the first two screens**). The formats you offer (a single class, a weekend, a residency) aren't on the page at all, even though the CMS already has an empty section waiting for them. Of 15 comparable Pilates, yoga and movement teacher pages reviewed, **only two showed organisers a "from" price and none had a proof section aimed at retreat hosts**, so closing those gaps would put you ahead of most of the field. Brands judge on different things (audience numbers, content samples, usage terms), and they already have their own option on your form, which is why a separate brand path will serve them better. No study measures how retreat hosts choose a visiting Pilates teacher. This report combines organiser hiring guides, general landing-page data (mostly from software companies and online shops) and what other teachers actually do, and it labels which is which.

**How to read the labels in this report**

| Label | What it means |
|---|---|
| *Measured* | A study or large dataset with numbers. Almost none of it comes from wellness businesses, so treat it as a direction, not a forecast. |
| *Advice* | Guidance from organisers, hiring guides, industry experts or companies selling tools. Opinion, sometimes self-interested. |
| *Examples* | What 15 comparable teacher pages and 8 creator brand pages show, read on 8 Nov 2026. This tells you what is normal, not what works. |
| *Judgement* | Our recommendation, reasoned from the above. You can disagree with these, and you should where you know your business better. |

## Every type of host runs the same four checks before enquiring

The clearest guidance comes from the people who hire teachers. BookRetreats tells teachers applying to retreat centres to **name their certification, insurance and real teaching experience**, to show they can teach experienced guests without losing beginners, to say how long they can stay, and to send a short teaching video, which it recommends *(advice)* ([BookRetreats](https://bookretreats.com/jobs/yoga-teacher)). A studio-software company's checklist for hiring a guest instructor adds three more: **references from previous hosts, which it calls more reliable than a strong CV or a social media following**; how the fee works (flat, a share of ticket sales, or both) plus lodging for teachers from out of town; and what equipment and room set-up the teacher needs *(advice)* ([Mariana Tek](https://www.marianatek.com/blog/8-questions-to-ask-before-finalizing-a-contract-with-a-guest-instructor/)).

Hotels and spas think in residencies. In a 2016 Spa Business panel, Six Senses described keeping a profile on each visiting practitioner with their **menu, references, documents and reviews**. Stays ran **10 to 14 days** at Kamalaya and up to a month at MSpa, and luxury spas charged guests about **US$150 to 300 an hour, often split 50/50** with the practitioner *(advice, ten years old)* ([Spa Business](https://www.spabusiness.com/spa-business-magazine/Ask-an-expert-Visiting-practitioners/31106)). The panel disagreed about social following: Kamalaya said it hardly matters because the programme serves guests already staying, while Como saw it as a way to bring new guests in. Hotels still run these as limited-time residencies on a published calendar *(examples)* ([Kempinski](https://www.kempinski.com/en/siam-hotel/luxury-kempinski-the-spa/wellness-expert-in-residence)).

Festivals want the short version: a 60 to 120 minute session, a bio of no more than 800 characters and ideally a teaching video (from a summary of a now-closed form) ([Bass Coast](https://basscoast.festivalpro.com/form/BuxdzSqnXnESfXSDVBKn/75)). An Irish festival also asks for insurance details and a tax clearance certificate ([Hullabaloo](https://www.offaly.ie/app/uploads/Hullabaloo-Facilitator-Brief-2025.pdf)). Many grassroots festivals **pay facilitators in tickets, not fees** ([festival application form](https://tally.so/r/XxOREz)) *(examples)*. Corporate buyers' guides tell HR teams to compare costs, ask for several references and call a random selection, and look for sessions adapted to the company *(advice, written for larger wellness companies rather than solo teachers)* ([IFEBP](https://blog.ifebp.org/choosing-a-wellness-vendor/); [Strive2bFit](https://strive2bfit.com/resources/how-to-choose-the-right-corporate-wellness-partners/)).

Put side by side, the four checks look like this.

| What hosts check | What answers it | Your page today |
|---|---|---|
| Will this suit our guests? | You teaching a real group on video; mixed-level, feel-good style; formats that fit their schedule | **Strong:** teaching video at the top, "Perfect for retreats that…" checklist. **Missing:** the formats you offer |
| Are you qualified and covered? | Certification, experience, insurance | **Strong** on BASI and 5,000+ hours. **Missing:** insurance |
| Who else has hired you? | Named hosts, quotes, photos of past events, references | **Weak:** no host quotes or event photos, and the logo strip sits below the form |
| What will it cost and involve? | Starting price, what's included, travel costs, how long you can stay, what you need on site | **Weak:** "from €150" is inside an FAQ answer at the bottom of the page; travel, stay length and equipment aren't covered |

*Judgement:* because retreats, festivals, hotels and teams all run these same checks, one page can serve them all, as long as each one sees itself named. Today the headline says "retreat or event", the checklist says "retreats that…" and the form offers Retreat, Event, Brand collaboration or Something else, so a hotel wellness manager or an HR lead has to work out whether you mean them. Fixing that takes a line or two, not a new page. Brands are the exception, covered in the last section.

## Three-quarters of attention lands on the first two screens

The most careful attention study available, by the usability firm Nielsen Norman Group, tracked 120 people's eyes across 130,000 glances. It found **57% of viewing time above the fold, 74% within the first two screens and 81% within the first three**. Within the first screen, most attention went to its top half *(measured, 2018, desktop screens)* ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)). Unbounce's analysis of 41,000 landing pages found **83% of visits came from phones**, and pages written in plain, simple language converted at **11.1% against 5.3%** for pages written at a professional level. Of the writing factors measured, long words had the strongest link with lower conversion *(measured, many industries, a pattern rather than proof of cause)* ([Unbounce](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/)). Your plain voice is already an advantage here.

On a phone, the first two screens of your page are the hero and the start of "Hi, I'm Chelsea". *Judgement:* that space has to tell a host what this is, that it's for them, where you work, one reason to trust you, and how to ask. Your hero does most of that already: the headline, the line about Lisbon, Europe and South Africa, the button, the BASI trust line and the video. Check it on your own phone. If the video pushes the button out of the first screen, the headline and button should come first.

Your 8 November change, putting About and credentials straight after the header, matches the strongest examples. Vanessa Michielon, the London teacher whose nervous-system positioning is closest to yours, puts a press logo strip directly under her hero ([Vanessa Michielon](https://www.vanessamichielon.com/)). ONEYOGAHOUSE in New York puts client logos before its form ([ONEYOGAHOUSE](https://oneyogahouse.com/corporate-yoga/)), and a Pilates creator's brand kit puts a stats bar right under the headline ([Movement by Mary](https://movementbymarybrandportfolio.lovable.app/)) *(examples)*. What is still too low on your page is everything after that. The logo strip sits below the form, and the price is inside the FAQ at the bottom, so a host who reaches the form knows who you are but not who has hired you or what it costs. *Judgement:* move the logos up beside the credentials, and bring the formats and prices above the form. A long page is fine for a paid, considered booking, where neither short nor long pages win outright ([CXL](https://cxl.com/long-form-or-short-form/)), as long as the deciding information comes first and the FAQ holds the detail.

One thing in the site's own code matters here. On a laptop, the sticky header keeps a "Request a proposal" button on screen the whole time. **On a phone, that button folds away into the menu**, so once a host scrolls past the hero, the only way to reach the form is to keep scrolling. Agency tests of a sticky button on long pages found lifts of at least 8% ([Conversion Rate Experts](https://conversion-rate-experts.com/sticky-cta-win-report/)) and 20.4% on a long phone homepage, along with a warning that it adds clutter where the button is already visible ([Convertibles](https://convertibles.dev/blogs/case-studies/homepage-sticky-cta-case-study)) *(examples, from shops rather than services)*. *Judgement:* a slim, quiet button on phones that hides once the form is on screen fits the site's calm style and fixes a real gap.

Many visitors will arrive from your outreach emails. Cold-email specialists advise that the page should **repeat the email's promise in its headline, proof and button**, with one main action ([LeadHaste](https://leadhaste.com/prompts/cold-email-landing-page-message-match); [LeadSharp](https://leadsharp.vercel.app/optimizing-outbound-landing-pages)) *(advice, no controlled study)*. You email retreats and studios, but the page never mentions studios. If those emails offer guest workshops, the page should say so too, in the hero line or in a format card.

## A visible price and named past events would set you apart

Nielsen Norman Group found that **price is the first thing prospective customers want to know** on any website, and advises firms with tailored services to show sample prices for a few typical cases, because early buyers are building a shortlist and only need to know the cost level *(advice from usability testing, 2006 and 2013)* ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/show-prices-for-common-scenarios/)). In a survey of software buyers, **81% wanted to find pricing on their own** and 71% said visible pricing made them more likely to buy *(measured, but by a company that sells pricing tools, and about software)* ([TrustRadius](https://solutions.trustradius.com/vendor-blog/tech-buyers-demand-honest-and-up-front-pricing-info/)). Among the teacher pages, only two show organisers a "from" price: Show Up Pilates lists workshops and events from $110, depending on the level of facilitation ([Show Up Pilates](https://showuppilatesevents.com/)), and Life in Movement in Portugal shows packages from €160 a day right next to its main button ([Life in Movement](https://www.lifeinmovement.co/portugal-private-pilates-retreat)). The rest say "get a quote" *(examples)*.

Your "Sessions start at €150" is a real advantage, but it is hidden and leaves the next questions open: per class or per day, and who pays for travel and accommodation? *Judgement:* show two or three typical cases with "from" prices and what's included, just above the form. The CMS can already do this. "Offers & prices" takes a Retreats & Events offer with a "From" price, a unit (session, workshop, day, programme) and a "What's included" list. The prices section currently appears below the form, so it would need moving up. Decide two money questions while you're there. Hotels may expect a revenue split rather than a flat fee, and festivals often pay in tickets. The current FAQ line, "with room to explore collaborations that feel meaningful and aligned", could be read as an openness to unpaid work, so it's worth deciding whether that's what you mean.

Proof is the bigger gap. Hosts trust other hosts more than a CV or a following ([Mariana Tek](https://www.marianatek.com/blog/8-questions-to-ask-before-finalizing-a-contract-with-a-guest-instructor/)), and visitors check what a business says about itself against what others say about it ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/topic/b2b-websites/)) *(advice)*. The most convincing proof among the teacher pages was Power of Pilates' run of dated cards, each headed with the partner's name: **Porsche Centre Reading in May 2026, four back-to-back sessions for Hewlett Packard Enterprise on International Women's Day, £2,400 raised for Breast Cancer Now** ([Power of Pilates](https://www.powerofpilates.co.uk/events.html)). These read as real, recent work in a way a logo strip can't. Testimonials that name the organisation (Meta and TD Bank on ONEYOGAHOUSE, Beauty Pie on [Chloe's Pilates](https://www.chloespilates.com/retreats-events)) beat first names alone *(examples)*. Marketing advice adds that quotes work best when they come from someone like the visitor, are specific and are recent ([Design Project](https://designproject.io/blog/incorporating-social-proof-testimonials-case-studies-and-logos/)). **None of the 15 pages gave retreat hosts their own proof section**, so a "Retreats and events I've taught" strip would fill a gap nobody else fills.

Your logo strip, shared with the home page, shows BASI, Insight Timer, Datchet Osteopath Clinic and Elf: a training body, a platform, a clinic and a brand. *Judgement:* a short label saying what the logos mean would stop a host assuming all four hired you for events, and a retreat or venue logo would mean more to hosts than any of them. Until you have host quotes, the CMS hides the "What hosts say" heading (checked in the site's code), so the page never shows an empty section. That matters, because an empty heading is one of the clearest signs of a neglected page ([Mill Studio](https://millstudiopilates.co.uk/corporate-and-brand)). In the meantime: use quotes from any hosts, studios or clinics you've taught for, add a line offering references from past hosts on request, and make collecting proof part of every 2027 booking. That means two lines and permission from the host on the last day, photos with guests' consent, and a card dated by season and year. Old dates hurt as much as no dates. Several teachers' pages still advertise 2021 courses or 2023 retreats *(examples)* ([Vanessa Michielon](https://www.vanessamichielon.com/corporate); [Life in Movement](https://www.lifeinmovement.co/)).

## The short form works; make it easier to reach and to forward

Most of the evidence on form length comes from companies that sell forms. One analysed 2 million business form submissions and found that **asking for a phone number cut conversion by 18.7%** ([Orbit Forms](https://orbitforms.ai/blog/b2b-form-conversion-best-practices)). Claims for multi-step forms range from 30% to 300% more submissions ([involve.me](https://www.involve.me/blog/lead-capture-form)), with a warning that lead quality can drop ([Pedowitz Group](https://www.pedowitzgroup.com/measure-multi-step-vs.-single-step-form-impact)) *(measured by vendors, treat with caution)*. Your form asks for no phone number, makes two of its four tap questions optional and sorts the request as it goes, so *judgement:* don't add questions. Do widen the "I'm planning" options so hotels and teams see themselves. ONEYOGAHOUSE asks for session type, Show Up Pilates routes enquiries with "What are you interested in?", and Alive in Cape Town asks for team size ([Alive](https://www.alive.capetown/corporatewellness)) *(examples)*.

Nielsen Norman Group's contact-page guidance says people still expect an email address and that forms shouldn't replace it *(advice, 2019)* ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/topic/b2b-websites/)). Some hosts would rather write their own email, especially when copying in a partner, so show your address beside the form. Keep the 48-hour promise. Businesses are slow: in a 2022 study of 114 business-to-business companies, **under 1% contacted a new enquiry within five minutes** *(measured, small sample)* ([Workato](https://workato.com/the-connector/lead-response-time-study)). ONEYOGAHOUSE also promises 48 hours and Show Up Pilates promises one to two business days *(examples)*. No study tests whether stating a reply time on the page raises enquiries. *Judgement:* promise only what you can keep while you're away teaching a retreat yourself. "Within 2 working days" is safer if travel makes 48 hours hard.

Adding a "book a call" calendar is low priority. The impressive figures come from booking-software firms working with sales teams that handle hundreds of forms a month, and reviewers note **little effect below about 100 enquiries a month** *(measured by vendors)* ([Ainora summary](https://ainora.lt/blog/b2b-meeting-booking-rate-every-study-2026)). Offering a 15-minute call in your reply does the same job.

What hosts do need is something to pass on. A retreat host often answers to a co-host, a hotel manager to a general manager, an HR lead to a budget holder. Nielsen Norman Group recommended a downloadable kit for exactly this "convince my boss" step *(advice, 2004)* ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/topic/b2b-websites/)). Six Senses keeps profiles of its visiting practitioners, and festivals ask for short bios. Sage Rountree, a yoga educator, offers bios in five lengths, a booking one-sheet and photo folders ([Sage Rountree](https://sagerountree.com/press/)), but **no Pilates page in the sample had a downloadable one-sheet** *(examples)*. *Judgement:* your paused rate-card one-sheets for studios, retreats and brands are the right tool here. The CMS already has a price setting for "Only in downloadable one-sheets", so private rates can live there and not on the page.

## Brands judge audience and samples, so give them their own path

Brands look for different things. Buffer's 2026 media-kit guide lists, in order: **key numbers, audience (age, gender, location), social links, about, testimonials, content samples and past collaborations, contact, then rates**, in two pages at most. An agency founder in the same piece says listing fees saves rounds of back-and-forth email *(advice)* ([Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/influencer-media-kit/)). Brands also check the numbers themselves. Modash, a tool brands use to find creators, shows each Pilates creator's followers, estimated fake followers, engagement rate and top audience countries. UK Pilates creators with 9,500 to 65,600 followers had **engagement rates between 2.1% and 5.4%**, apart from one outlier *(measured, platform data)* ([Modash](https://www.modash.io/find-influencers/united-kingdom/pilates)). So the numbers on your page must match what the tools show, and the page should add what they can't see: YouTube, Insight Timer, your BASI training and your stress, pain and nervous-system niche. Brands pass on outdated numbers, audiences that don't fit their market, and creators promoting too many brands at once *(advice, vendor checklists)* ([ContentGrip](https://www.contentgrip.com/how-to-vet-influencers/); [Toptal](https://www.toptal.com/creator/post/how-to-build-an-influencer-media-kit)).

The bar is low. Of eight wellness creator and studio brand pages read in full, **none showed rates, audience details or a media kit, and only one showed a follower number** *(examples)*. Most teachers who serve both audiences keep them apart: Yoga Jawn sends brands and venues to different pages ([Yoga Jawn](https://www.yogajawn.com/brand-partnerships)), Chloe's Pilates and Vanessa Michielon split them across their menus, and others use one page with clearly labelled sections and a form that routes the request ([Alyssa Moyer](https://www.pilatesalyssa.com/); [Show Up Pilates](https://showuppilatesevents.com/)) *(examples)*. No data shows which converts better. Your own site already follows the same logic for private clients and organisers: your site notes say "two audiences, two pages, so neither reads the other's offer". *Judgement:* the same reasoning applies to brands. A retreat host shouldn't have to read about usage rights, and a brand shouldn't have to read about group sizes and travel.

There is a fair case for staying on one page. It's simpler to keep up, live classes at brand events sit in both camps, and **a thin brand page with no samples or current numbers would look worse than none**. *Judgement:* do it in two steps. For now, add a short, labelled "For brands" band low on the main page (what you make, a starting price and a link or email), and keep "Brand collaboration" on the form. Once you have three or more content samples and up-to-date numbers, build a brand page with those numbers and the date you updated them, the samples, packages with usage terms and its own form questions (brand and website, goal, what they want made, platforms, paid-ad use, timeline, budget range). A bridge between the two: small teachers win brand partners through retreats, for example branded gifts for guests ([Rada Yoga](https://radayoga.substack.com/p/youre-never-too-small-for-meaningful)), so link the two paths to each other. Keep teams and brands apart in your copy, too. *Judgement:* pages that lump "corporate and brand" together blur who each paragraph is for.

On price, your private rate of **€300 for one short-form video with six months of organic use** is about £260 at recent rates (not checked live). UK guides put one organic-only video at roughly **£150 to £350 for experienced creators**, and one includes three months of organic use as standard ([Fluxnote](https://fluxnote.io/guides/ugc-content-rates-uk); [After Hours Creator Club](https://afterhourscreatorclub.com/guides/how-much-to-charge-as-a-ugc-creator-in-2025/)). Marketplace averages are lower, around US$154 to $198 ([Net Influencer on Collabstr](https://www.netinfluencer.com/influencer-collab-costs-in-decline-but-ugc-offers-bright-spot-in-2025-report-finds/)), and paid-ad use usually adds 30% to 50% ([Influencer Metric](https://influencermetric.com/blog/ugc-rates-2026-us-uk-gulf)) *(advice from vendors, no independent survey)*. That puts your rate in the middle-to-upper range, with generous usage. In an October 2026 panel of 33 industry professionals, the most common view was to **publish a starting point or range and negotiate the rest** *(advice)* ([Net Influencer](https://www.netinfluencer.com/should-creator-rates-be-standardized-and-published-33-industry-professionals-weigh-in/)). *Judgement:* "from €300" on the brand path is defensible. Keep the full rate card private, and consider making three months your standard usage with an extension fee.

## Conclusion

The page's real job is to get you onto a shortlist and survive being forwarded. It doesn't have to close the sale. The organiser evidence describes a buyer comparing a few teachers and often answering to someone else, so success means a host can find a price case, a quote from a host like them and a one-sheet to send on within two screens. That is a different goal from persuading a private client, and it explains why price and proof matter more on this page than polish.

The biggest gap is a habit, not a design problem. Layout changes can move what you already have, but only events create new proof, so the page will get stronger at the pace your 2027 bookings leave behind a named quote, photos and a dated card. Brand growth depends on a different set of material (samples and current numbers) that needs its own routine. Because almost no one in this niche shows prices, formats or host quotes, a modest effort on both will stand out more than it would in most markets.

## Questions only you can answer

**Prices and money**

1. Which two or three typical cases should show a price (for example a single class, a half-day or weekend, a week-long retreat or residency), and what is the "from" price for each?
2. What does €150 buy today: one class, one hour, one day?
3. Do hosts pay for your travel, accommodation and meals, and does that change for Lisbon, the rest of Europe and South Africa?
4. Will you work on a flat fee, a revenue share (common in hotel spas), or either?
5. Will you take festival slots paid in tickets, or only paid sessions? And what did you mean by "room to explore collaborations that feel meaningful and aligned"?

**Proof**

6. Which past retreats, events, venues, studios and companies can you name, with month and year?
7. Who would give you a two-line quote with their name, role and organisation, and do you have photos you're allowed to use?
8. Are you happy to offer references from past hosts on request?
9. What does each logo on the strip stand for (trained with, taught for, featured on), and should any retreat or venue logos be added?

**Your offer and audiences**

10. Which formats do you actually offer, and how long can you stay at one place?
11. Are studios a target for guest workshops? Your outreach emails go to them, but the page doesn't mention them.
12. Should corporate teams and hotels be named in the hero, or only lower down?
13. Which side earns more per day of your time in 2027, retreats and events or brand work? That decides which leads.
14. Are you willing to promote a host's event to your YouTube and Instagram audience, and should the page say so?

**Brands**

15. Do you want a separate brand page now, or a short band first and a page once you have samples and current numbers?
16. Which brand work can you show (links or clips), and what are your current Instagram and other numbers and top audience countries?
17. Should "from €300" appear publicly, or stay on the rate card only? Is six months of organic use your standard, or would three months plus an extension fee suit you better?
18. If brands get their own page, what should the menu say instead of "Retreats & collabs"?

**Practicalities and promises**
